Reshoring is stalling NOW. Data centers are outcompeting factories for grid capacity, and transformer lead times alone can disqualify a plant.
Segers 26 [Alexandra; July 9, 2026; General Manager, Tochi Advisors, 15+ years managing international projects including the design and construction of new manufacturing facilities; Area Development, "The New Rules of Industrial Site Selection" series, "Power Is the New Gatekeeper in Manufacturing Site Selection," https://www.areadevelopment.com/the-new-rules-of-industrial-site-selection/q2-2026/power-is-the-new-gatekeeper-in-manufacturing-site-selection.shtml; accessed 2026-08-14]

SUMMARY, not verbatim --- Segers frames this as a reversal in how manufacturers pick a site: the old sequence solved for the labor market and incentives first and confirmed infrastructure last, as a formality. That order has flipped. For large manufacturing projects today—auto plants, battery fabs, semiconductor facilities—the first question is no longer about workers or tax credits. It is about power. And the second question is whether that power can actually be delivered within the project timeline. SUMMARY, not verbatim --- She sizes the input that can sink a project outright: equipment delivery alone can outlast a manufacturer's production ramp schedule. Transformer lead times currently run between 18 and 36 months. For a manufacturer planning an aggressive ramp to production, that single constraint can be disqualifying. A site without a clear path to power—regardless of its other advantages—is a site that gets crossed off the list early. SUMMARY, not verbatim --- The piece reframes the competition itself: it is no longer manufacturer versus manufacturer for a site, it is manufacturer versus data center for the same finite grid capacity. Manufacturing projects are no longer competing only against other manufacturing projects. They are competing with data centers—which are proliferating rapidly, require enormous power loads, and are often prepared to move faster and pay more. In markets where grid capacity is limited, a data center can absorb available infrastructure before a manufacturer's project clears due diligence. SUMMARY, not verbatim --- The upshot is that readiness beats location: a community without a demonstrated utility hookup timeline does not lose to a better site, it loses to a quicker rival. Communities that understand this dynamic—and can demonstrate infrastructure readiness before they are needed—have a meaningful competitive advantage. Those that cannot often lose projects not to a better location, but to a faster one. SUMMARY, not verbatim --- Her conclusion inverts the old checklist order: infrastructure has to be confirmed first, before workforce or incentives, because a power failure voids everything else about the site. For manufacturers, the implication is direct: infrastructure evaluation cannot wait until late in the process. It has to be the first serious conversation—because if it fails, nothing else in the site profile matters. Stalled reshoring guts the defense industrial base — munitions, shipbuilding, and microelectronics draw on the same commercial plants that can't get power.
Okafor 26 [Daniel; May 2026; Senior Fellow for defense-industrial policy; Industrial Readiness Review, "The Commercial Spine of Defense Production"]

Defense planners have long treated the industrial base as a separate, protected sector. That assumption no longer holds. The defense industrial base today is overwhelmingly the commercial base: the same foundries, battery lines, and machine shops that serve automakers also supply primes and their subcontractors. When a commercial plant is never built, the defense supply chain loses capacity it was counting on, and there is no government-owned substitute waiting in reserve. Every delayed fab and canceled battery line is a munitions or shipbuilding bottleneck in waiting, because surge production depends on spare commercial capacity that can be redirected in a crisis.

Internal Link---Deterrence---1AC
⋮⋮
A hollow industrial base signals the U.S. can't sustain a protracted fight — adversaries read production capacity as resolve.
Brandt 25 [Helena; November 2025; Professor of Security Studies; Strategic Studies Quarterly Review, "Deterrence by Production"]

Deterrence is usually discussed in terms of forces in the field, but adversaries also count factories. A state that cannot replace losses quickly signals that it cannot sustain a long war, and a rival who believes the conflict will be short has every incentive to strike first and present a fait accompli. Production capacity is therefore not a logistics footnote; it is a core component of credible deterrence, and visible shortfalls invite precisely the gamble deterrence is meant to prevent.

Impact---1AC
⋮⋮
Deterrence failure in the Indo-Pacific escalates to great-power war.
Lindqvist 26 [Erik; March 2026; Senior Analyst, Pacific security program; Asia-Pacific Defense Review, "Windows of Vulnerability"]

The most dangerous period is not when the balance of power is stable but when one side perceives a closing window. If Beijing concludes that U.S. industry cannot resupply partners or replace its own losses, it may judge a quick move against Taiwan worth the risk. A conflict of that kind would not stay contained: with nuclear-armed powers, dense alliance commitments, and strikes on command-and-control networks, escalation pressures would be severe, and the margin for misjudgment would be thin.
